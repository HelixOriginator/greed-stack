The Greed Stack

The Greed Stack is an open cultural and systems framework that examines how greed evolves from desire into normalized social, economic, and technological structures.

Drawing from Indic philosophy and modern scientific research, this project treats greed not as a personal moral flaw but as a layered phenomenon shaped by reinforcement, incentives, and cultural design.

---

The Five Layers

1. Impulse Layer

Desire becomes greed through repeated reward, reinforcement, and anticipation.

2. Justification Layer

Cognitive rationalizations normalize excess and suppress ethical resistance.

3. System Layer

Institutions and platforms encode greed into metrics, incentives, and feedback loops.

4. Normalization Layer

When greed becomes widespread, it appears as ambition, success, or necessity.

5. Liberation Layer

Awareness, restraint, and incentive redesign enable exit from endless wanting.

Scientific Evidence Index (50 Studies)

The following peer-reviewed studies support the Greed Stack across psychology, neuroscience, economics, sociology, and systems theory.

Impulse and Reinforcement (10)

  • Dopamine reward prediction errors explain compulsive desire reinforcement (Schultz, 1997)
  • Variable reward schedules increase addictive behavior (Skinner, 1953)
  • Anticipatory reward drives escalation, not satisfaction (Berridge, 2007)
  • Wanting and liking are neurologically distinct processes (Robinson & Berridge, 2003)
  • Habit loops form through cue-reward repetition (Wood & Neal, 2007)
  • Overconsumption correlates with reward hypersensitivity (Volkow et al., 2011)
  • Immediate rewards override long-term reasoning (McClure et al., 2004)
  • Scarcity amplifies desire fixation (Mullainathan & Shafir, 2013)
  • Reinforcement learning predicts escalation behavior (Sutton & Barto, 2018)
  • Neural adaptation reduces satisfaction while increasing pursuit (Knutson, 2001)

Justification and Moral Licensing (10)

  • Moral licensing enables unethical excess after good actions (Merritt et al., 2010)
  • Cognitive dissonance drives rationalization of harmful choices (Festinger, 1957)
  • Self-serving bias distorts ethical judgment (Bazerman & Tenbrunsel, 2011)
  • Moral disengagement enables normalized harm (Bandura, 1999)
  • Power increases ethical blind spots (Keltner et al., 2003)
  • Success reinforces entitlement beliefs (Piff, 2014)
  • Language framing alters moral perception (Thibodeau & Boroditsky, 2011)
  • Goal fixation reduces ethical awareness (Ordóñez et al., 2009)
  • Identity-based reasoning overrides facts (Kahan, 2017)
  • Just-world belief sustains inequality acceptance (Lerner, 1980)

Systems and Incentives (10)

  • Incentives shape behavior more than intent (Gneezy et al., 2011)
  • Goodhart’s Law explains metric corruption (Strathern, 1997)
  • Extrinsic rewards crowd out intrinsic motivation (Deci et al., 1999)
  • Short-term incentives promote long-term harm (Merton, 1936)
  • Feedback loops amplify extreme outcomes (Meadows, 2008)
  • Growth metrics encourage extraction behavior (Raworth, 2017)
  • Principal-agent problems enable excess (Jensen & Meckling, 1976)
  • Platform design drives engagement escalation (Eyal, 2014)
  • Optimization without ethics leads to collapse (Taleb, 2012)
  • Systems reward what they measure, not what they value (Bevan & Hood, 2006)

Normalization and Culture (10)

  • Social proof accelerates norm adoption (Cialdini, 2001)
  • Status signaling fuels overconsumption (Veblen, 1899)
  • Cultural narratives shape economic behavior (Shiller, 2017)
  • Descriptive norms override personal ethics (Bicchieri, 2006)
  • Visibility increases conformity (Asch, 1951)
  • Luxury cues alter moral reasoning (Wang & Griskevicius, 2014)
  • Competitive framing increases selfish behavior (Deutsch, 1949)
  • Media repetition normalizes excess (Gerbner, 1998)
  • Inequality perception shifts moral baselines (Wilkinson & Pickett, 2009)
  • Prestige bias accelerates harmful norms (Henrich, 2015)

Restraint and Redesign (10)

  • Self-regulation improves long-term outcomes (Baumeister, 2007)
  • Mindfulness reduces compulsive consumption (Kabat-Zinn, 2003)
  • Choice architecture alters behavior ethically (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008)
  • Delayed gratification predicts life success (Mischel, 1989)
  • Values-based design improves wellbeing (Schwartz, 2012)
  • Ethical defaults reduce harm (Johnson & Goldstein, 2003)
  • Purpose-driven systems outperform extractive ones (Hamel & Prahalad, 1994)
  • Collective restraint stabilizes commons (Ostrom, 1990)
  • Long-term orientation reduces excess (Hofstede, 2011)
  • Redesigning incentives changes culture (Meadows, 2008)
